The Burj Khalifa is a skyscraper of mixed use, with residences, offices and a hotel. When it opened it
was the tallest building and the tallest man-made structure in the world, and it overtook Taipei 101
(about 509 m), which had held the building record until then.

## Section 2: Why the Tower Was Built

Economic diversification. Dubai has far smaller oil reserves than its neighbour Abu Dhabi, and from
the late 1990s its leadership pursued growth through trade, tourism, aviation, finance and real estate.
Landmark projects, such as the sail-shaped Burj Al Arab hotel and the Palm Jumeirah island, were part
of this strategy. A record-breaking tower was the next step.
Putting Dubai on the map. An executive from Nakheel Properties recalled that Dubai's ruler, Sheikh
Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum, wanted to put the emirate on the map with something truly
sensational. Officials also argued that projects of this kind were needed to win international recognition
and, with it, foreign investment.
The centrepiece of a new district. The tower was designed as the anchor of Downtown Dubai
(originally "Downtown Burj Dubai"), a large mixed-use development planned to include around 30,000
homes, parkland, a large artificial lake, and the Dubai Mall, one of the world's largest shopping centres.
The tower was meant to lift the value of everything around it.
A planned first. In its early planning the tower was imagined as entirely residential. It later became a
mix of residences (including Armani Residences), offices and a hotel, and the finishes were upgraded as
the project progressed.
Why it was renamed. The tower was always intended to carry the name "Burj Dubai". The name
changed on opening day for reasons tied to the global financial crisis; see Section 9.

## Section 3: Design and Planning

The design was produced by Skidmore, Owings & Merrill, the Chicago firm that had also designed the
Sears (now Willis) Tower, a former world-record holder. Adrian Smith led the architecture, and Bill
Baker led the structural engineering.
Inspiration. The three-lobed, Y-shaped floor plan is credited to the Hymenocallis, a desert flower, and
to patterns in Islamic architecture. Smith has said that he found a prototype for the plan in the curved
three-wing layout of Lake Point Tower in Chicago, visible from his office window. Reports also point to
Tower Palace Three, a residential building in Seoul, as a structural precedent.
Buttressed core. Wind was the dominant design problem. SOM developed a "buttressed core" system:
a six-sided central core that resists twisting, flanked by three wings that resist wind shear and brace one
another. The wings step back in a spiral as they rise, which disrupts wind vortices. More than 40
wind-tunnel tests were run (at the RWDI laboratory in Guelph, Canada), and even the construction
cranes were tested.
Design changes. The architect reportedly disliked the first design of the upper part of the tower, and
Adrian Smith left SOM in 2006 to found his own firm, before the tower topped out.

## Section 4: When Construction Started

Excavation began in January 2004, with the first bored piles started in February 2004. Sources differ
on the exact day: some give 6 January 2004, others 12 January 2004, and others cite 21 September 2004
as the date on which the main contractors formally began or a groundbreaking was marked. The
best-supported reading is that site work started in January 2004 and that the main vertical construction
(the superstructure) began in March 2005.
The foundation work was substantial. The tower rests on a reinforced-concrete mat supported by 192
bored piles. The piles are about 1.5 m in diameter, and published lengths range from roughly 43 m to
about 50 m. A high-density, low-permeability concrete was specified, and a cathodic protection system
was installed under the mat because of corrosive chemicals in Dubai's groundwater.

## Section 7: Key Events During Construction

The Record-Breaking Climb
The tower broke three records on its way up. In July 2007 it passed Taipei 101 to become the tallest
building. In September 2007 it passed the CN Tower as the tallest free-standing structure. In April 2008
it surpassed a television mast in North Dakota to become the tallest man-made structure ever built. By
the time the spire was finished in January 2009, the tower stood roughly 320 m taller than Taipei 101.
Labour Unrest, March 2006
On the night of 21 March 2006, around 2,500 workers employed by the Dubai firm Al Naboodah Laing
O'Rourke, on buildings and housing around the Burj Dubai site, rioted over pay, unpaid overtime and
treatment by foremen. They damaged cars, offices, computers and machinery, with damage estimated at
about US$1 million. The workers stopped work the next day too, and the Emirates' labour authorities
intervened. The main tower joint venture said the disturbances took place on the outskirts and not in the
tower's own construction area, and that the tower's progress was unaffected. Soon afterwards the
government announced it would allow construction unions.
Strike, October 2007
On 27 October 2007, more than 4,500 workers on the Burj Dubai, most of them Indian, downed tools
over pay and conditions, and the dispute continued into November. The owners later said the strike was
over.
The Concrete-Pumping Record, November 2007
In November 2007, high-strength concrete (80 megapascal) was pumped from ground level to a height
of 601 m, breaking the previous record held for a building by Taipei 101 (470 m) and the overall
vertical-pumping record of 532 m. Pressure during pumping was nearly 200 bar.
Delay for Upgraded Finishes, June 2008
On 9 June 2008, Emaar announced that completion would slip to September 2009. A spokesperson
explained that the luxury finishes chosen in 2004, when the tower was first conceived, were being
replaced with upgraded ones.
The Global Financial Crisis, 2008 to 2010
The tower's later stages unfolded as Dubai's property boom collapsed. Many projects were suspended or
abandoned, but the Burj kept going. By late 2009 Dubai was in deep financial trouble, with debts
reported at around US$100 billion at the state-linked level, and Abu Dhabi provided about US$25
billion of direct and indirect support in 2009, including a US$10 billion rescue in December.

## Section 8: Engineering Highlights

Feature
Detail
Structural system
Buttressed core: hexagonal central core with three wings in a Y plan.
Material
Reinforced concrete up to about level 156; structural steel in the spire above.
Foundation
Concrete mat on 192 bored piles, each about 1.5 m wide, roughly 43 to 50 m deep.
Concrete in foundation
Nearly 45,000 cubic metres, weighing over 110,000 tonnes.
Concrete pumping
Two of the world's largest pumps; pumped to 601 m, a world record at the time.
Facade
Roughly 26,000 individually cut glass panels; the highest installation of an
aluminium and glass facade in the world, at 512 m.
Wind testing
More than 40 wind-tunnel tests; setbacks tuned to confuse wind vortices.
Spire
Telescopic spire that is part of the structure and houses communications equipment.
Safety
Fireproof concrete-reinforced stairwells and refuge floors.
Dubai's heat made concrete placement a particular challenge: the mixes had to resist separating under
the huge pumping pressure and avoid setting too quickly in summer temperatures. Pours were often
done at night, and ice was used to cool the mix.

## Section 9: Completion, Opening and Renaming

Exterior completion. The exterior of the structure was complete on 1 October 2009, after the cladding
had been finished in September 2009.
Opening ceremony, 4 January 2010. The tower was opened in a ceremony staged by Prisme
International. A 72,000-watt light projector lit the tower, fountains and fireworks depicted the
Hymenocallis flower, and a lighting sequence imitating a beating heart marked the construction phase.
Spotlights reached about 10 km into the sky, and the show ended with fireworks. Screens revealed the
tower's long-secret height to the crowd for the first time: 828 m.
The renaming. At the ceremony, Dubai's ruler unexpectedly announced that the tower would be called
Burj Khalifa, after Sheikh Khalifa bin Zayed Al Nahyan, ruler of Abu Dhabi and President of the UAE.
Commentators widely read the gesture as thanks for Abu Dhabi's financial rescue of Dubai during the
debt crisis.
Unfinished interiors. Much of the building was still unfinished inside at the opening. The tower
became fully operational on 23 January 2011 with the opening of the last major planned facility.
Records. On completion it was the world's tallest building, tallest free-standing structure and tallest
man-made structure. It also held records for the highest installation of an aluminium and glass facade,
and for the highest vertical concrete pumping.

## Section 11: The Workforce

Building the tower took more than 22 million man-hours from about 12,000 professionals and skilled
workers and more than 60 consultants and contracting companies from around the world, according to
the project's published figures. The great majority of manual workers were migrants from South Asia, as
in the wider UAE construction industry.
Their conditions drew sustained criticism. A Human Rights Watch report of November 2006 on
construction workers in the UAE described unpaid or very low wages, debt to recruitment agencies, the
withholding of passports and hazardous working conditions. That report put the average migrant
construction worker's earnings at about US$175 a month. The Dubai government rejected accusations of
systematic injustice, but announced measures in March 2006 to allow construction unions. Workers'
protests in 2006 and 2007, described in Section 7, are part of the tower's recorded history, and any
complete account of the project includes them alongside the engineering.

## Section 12: Notes on Sources and Conflicting Dates

The facts above are drawn from Emaar's official Burj Khalifa website, Wikipedia's article on the tower and its
labour-force article, Associated Press and NBC News reporting on the opening, Reuters and Khaleej Times coverage of
the 2006 unrest, a Mail & Guardian analysis of the opening, the Swarthmore Global Nonviolent Action Database,
Arabian Business's construction feature, Concrete Products magazine, MEED, and a set of project fact sheets.
Where sources conflict: (1) The start date is given variously as 6 January, 12 January and 21 September 2004. (2)
Some pages state that the opening was when "the final steel beam was installed"; that claim is not supported by better
sources, which date topping out to January 2009. (3) Piles are reported between about 43 m and 50 m long depending
on whether the tower or the podium is meant. (4) Costs are reported as about US$1.5 billion; the broader Downtown
development cost far more. The figures in this document use the best-supported version in each case.
Please verify any figure you plan to cite in formal work against Emaar's own published material or the Council on Tall
Buildings and Urban Habitat (CTBUH).
